Sick day rules for diabetes

By Vishnu Purushothaman, who makes Companion · Checked against the sources below on October 19, 2026

Contact your care team or emergency services straight away if you're vomiting and can't keep fluids down, have moderate or high ketones, are breathing fast, have stomach pain, or are getting drowsy or confused.

Illness usually raises glucose, even if you're eating less. The standard rules: keep taking insulin, drink plenty of fluids, check glucose more often, and with type 1 diabetes check ketones. Some tablets, such as metformin and SGLT2 inhibitors, are usually paused while you can't keep fluids down, and your care team's sick-day plan should say which of yours.

Why being ill pushes readings up

An infection, a fever or even a bad cold raises stress hormones, and those tell the liver to release glucose. That's why readings can climb on a day you've barely eaten, and why stopping insulin because you're not eating is the most dangerous mistake on a sick day.

The rules most care teams give

  • Keep taking insulin, even if you eat less. Your care team may adjust the amount; don't stop it.
  • Drink plenty: small sips often if you're struggling, unless you've been told to limit fluids.
  • Check more often: every two to four hours, including overnight.
  • Check ketones if you have type 1 diabetes, or if your care team has asked you to.
  • Keep some carbohydrate going if you can't eat normally: soup, toast, juice or regular soda in sips.

Tablets that are often paused

Some medicines are usually stopped for a day or two while you're vomiting, have diarrhoea or can't keep fluids down, because dehydration makes them risky: metformin and SGLT2 inhibitors (dapagliflozin, empagliflozin, canagliflozin) among them, and some blood-pressure medicines. Which of yours, and when to restart, is for your care team's plan; don't stop or start anything on a guess.

When to call your care team the same day

  • You can't keep fluids down, or you've vomited more than twice.
  • Readings stay above about 250 mg/dL (13.9 mmol/L).
  • Ketones are moderate or high.
  • You're not sure what to do.

Write your plan down before you need it

A sick day is the worst time to work out the rules. Ask your care team for a written sick-day plan at your next appointment: which tablets to pause, when to check ketones, and who to call.
